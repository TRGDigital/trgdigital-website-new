Medication errors are one of the most serious risks in any care setting. An omission, a double-dose, a wrong-resident administration, each has potentially life-altering consequences. Paper MAR charts have served the sector for decades, but their limitations are increasingly hard to defend when better alternatives exist.

Electronic Medication Administration Records (eMAR) have moved from a nice-to-have to an operational standard in the most progressive parts of the sector. Here's what the shift actually looks like in practice.

## The problems with paper MARs

Paper MAR charts work when they're completed correctly, filed accurately, and reviewed regularly. In practice, that relies entirely on staff discipline and management oversight, with no safety net for human error.

Common issues include:

- **Unexplained blanks**: staff forget to sign, or sign late, and by audit time, it's impossible to confirm whether a dose was given
- **Illegibility**: handwritten entries can be misread, particularly for dose quantities
- **Transcription errors**: when charts are rewritten at the start of each month, errors are introduced
- **No real-time alerts**: paper can't flag when a medication round is running late or hasn't been completed

<Callout variant="warning">
Paper MAR charts with unexplained gaps are consistently flagged in CQC inspection reports as a concern. Even a well-run service can receive a Requires Improvement rating on Safe if medication records have gaps, regardless of whether any actual harm occurred.
</Callout>

## What eMAR actually does

A good eMAR system replaces the paper chart with a digital interface that guides staff through each medication round. The care worker sees the resident's photograph, the medication list, and confirms or records a reason for any deviation, in real time, at the point of administration.

This creates several advantages:

**Timestamped, auditable records.** Every administration, or omission with reason, is recorded with a timestamp and the staff member's ID. There are no blanks, and retrospective editing is visible in the audit trail.

**Alerts and escalation.** If a medication round hasn't been completed within its scheduled window, the system can alert the nurse on duty or the registered manager. No more discovering a missed round at the end of a shift.

**Stock management.** eMAR systems can track consumption and flag when a resident is running low on supply, reducing the emergency pharmacy calls that disrupt every medication manager's working week.

**Handover quality.** The outgoing shift can see at a glance what was administered, what was refused, and what's outstanding. Information doesn't get lost between shifts.

<PullQuote>An eMAR system doesn't make clinical decisions, but it makes it much harder for good clinical decisions to be poorly documented.</PullQuote>

## The rollout challenge

The barrier to eMAR adoption isn't usually cost or technology, it's change management. Staff who have administered medication from paper charts for years find the transition uncomfortable, particularly older workers less confident with technology.

The services that roll out eMAR successfully treat the change as a human project, not a technology project. They identify a champion on each floor or unit, someone whose confidence with the system influences their colleagues. They run parallel systems (paper alongside digital) for a handover period rather than a hard cutover. And they frame the change around the benefit to residents, not the benefit to auditors.

<Callout variant="tip">
Budget for training time properly. A rushed eMAR implementation that leaves staff uncertain leads to workarounds and poor adoption. Two weeks of supported practice is worth more than the cost of the time.
</Callout>

## What to look for in a system

Not all eMAR solutions are equal. When evaluating options, look for:

- Integration with your existing care management software (or a plan to integrate)
- Offline capability, medication rounds happen even when Wi-Fi drops
- Resident photo prompt on every administration screen (prevents wrong-resident errors)
- Audit trail that cannot be edited without logging the original entry
- Support for controlled drug recording, ideally with witness signature flow
- Good mobile UX, if the device is awkward to use, staff will find workarounds
