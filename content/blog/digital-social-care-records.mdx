The NHS Long Term Plan made it clear: digital social care records are not optional. The government has set an expectation that all CQC-registered adult social care providers will move to digital care records, and funding has been made available through local ICSs (Integrated Care Systems) to support the transition.

If your service is still on paper, the question isn't whether to make the move, it's how, and when.

## What's actually being asked

A Digital Social Care Record (DSCR) is, at its simplest, a digital version of the care record that currently exists on paper: care plans, daily notes, risk assessments, and the evidence of care delivered. The government's DSCR programme isn't mandating a specific product, it's requiring that providers use a system on the approved supplier list, which ensures minimum standards around data security, interoperability, and records quality.

The driver behind this isn't bureaucracy. It's the recognition that care records held in paper files can't communicate with GP systems, hospital discharge teams, or the wider health and social care network. As integrated care becomes the model, the ability to share information safely and quickly is foundational.

## The funding picture

Most ICSs have received ring-fenced funding to support DSCR adoption among care providers in their area. In practice, this means grants or subsidised licences for approved systems, but the availability and scale of this funding varies significantly by geography and has been time-limited.

If you haven't already engaged with your local ICS or Digital Social Care lead, do so now. Waiting until funding windows close to start the conversation is one of the most common and avoidable mistakes providers make.

<PullQuote>The providers who move early get the best support, the most funding, and the smoothest implementation. The ones who wait until the deadline scramble.</PullQuote>

## What inspectors are expecting

CQC has been clear that digital capabilities are increasingly factored into assessments. Under the single assessment framework, the quality statement on "Learning, improvement and innovation" references digital literacy and the use of digital tools to improve care quality. While not having a digital system won't automatically lead to a poor rating, having one, and using it well, is increasingly a positive indicator in Well-Led assessments.

More practically: digital records allow inspectors to review evidence quickly and comprehensively. A care home with 40 residents and complete digital records can have an inspector review 12 months of a resident's care documentation in minutes. The same exercise with paper files takes hours.

## Making the transition

The biggest barrier is usually staff anxiety, not technology. Care workers who have been completing paper records for years are often apprehensive about digital systems, concerned that they'll be slower, more complicated, or expose gaps in their literacy.

The reality is usually the reverse. Most staff find digital recording faster once they're past the initial learning curve, and the prompts and structure in a good system actually make the quality of records better, not worse.

For a successful transition:

1. **Choose early adopters thoughtfully.** Identify staff who are comfortable with technology and willing to champion the change. Their confidence is infectious.
2. **Run parallel systems during the transition.** Don't cut off paper records immediately. A handover period reduces the risk of records gaps.
3. **Get residents and families onboard.** Many residents will appreciate being shown the digital record, seeing that their care is being recorded well is reassuring.
4. **Use the audit trail.** One of the biggest advantages of digital records is the ability to audit completeness and quality. Use it to identify where extra support or training is needed.

## The interoperability piece

Beyond compliance, the longer-term value of digital records lies in integration. A DSCR that can connect with your local GP practice, share discharge summaries with the hospital, and receive alerts from the pharmacy is a fundamentally different operational tool from a standalone digital file.

This interoperability is still nascent in most of the sector, but the architecture is being built. The providers who adopt compliant, standards-based digital records now will be best placed to benefit from integrated care as it matures.
