Every registered manager has felt it, the notification lands, and for a moment the stomach drops. CQC inspections are no one's idea of fun. But the managers who handle them with the most confidence aren't necessarily the ones running the most perfect services. They're the ones who know exactly where everything is.

That's what digital readiness is actually about. Not paperwork for its own sake, but being able to answer any question, immediately, calmly, with evidence.

## What inspectors are actually looking for

The CQC's single assessment framework evaluates services across five key questions: Are they safe? Effective? Caring? Responsive? Well-led? The quality statements under each of these increasingly reference digital capability, not because technology is the point, but because it's one of the clearest signals of a well-organised, evidence-driven service.

An inspector asking to see the last three months of medication administration records wants evidence. Whether you produce a neat digital report or a lever-arch folder of paper MAR charts, the evidence is what matters. But the digital report takes thirty seconds to pull. The lever-arch folder takes thirty minutes to find.

## The checklist

Here's what to have in order before inspection day.

### Care records

- Every resident has a current, completed care plan that has been reviewed within the last 12 weeks
- Risk assessments are present, dated, and reflect current presentation
- Handover notes and daily records are complete with no unexplained gaps
- Any safeguarding concerns are documented and show evidence of follow-up

### Medication

- eMAR records (or paper MARs) show no unexplained gaps or blanks
- PRN protocols are in place for all as-needed medications
- Controlled drug register is complete and reconciled
- Last pharmacy/GP medication review dates are documented

<Callout variant="tip">
If you're still on paper MARs, the single most impactful digital change you can make before an inspection is moving to an eMAR system. Auditable, timestamped records remove a significant source of inspector concern at a stroke.
</Callout>

### Staffing

- Rota records show minimum staffing ratios were maintained across the inspection window
- Dependency tool data supports staffing levels
- Agency use is documented with reason codes
- All staff files include valid DBS, references, and induction sign-off

### Governance

- The last three registered manager audits are available with action plans
- Any complaints have a documented response and resolution
- Incidents are logged with evidence of lessons-learned review
- DP data is processing correctly (Subject Access Requests, ICO registration)

### Well-led

- Staff supervision records are current
- Team meeting minutes from the last 12 months are available
- Resident and family survey results with any action taken
- Quality improvement plan is active and reviewed at least quarterly

## What not to do the week before

Resist the urge to retrospectively complete or amend records. Inspectors are experienced at spotting inconsistencies, and a cluster of record completions in the days before an inspection raises more questions than a gap does. If records are incomplete, acknowledge it and demonstrate your plan to address it.

Don't attempt to bury concerns by reorganising your files. If there's an open safeguarding, a pattern of incidents, or a staffing challenge, have your narrative ready. Inspectors respond well to honest, reflective accounts that show learning.

## Building a system, not a sprint

The providers who consistently achieve Good and Outstanding ratings aren't the ones who sprint into shape before each inspection. They maintain a rolling state of readiness. That means regular internal audits, a live complaints register, team meetings that are actually minuted, and supervision records that are kept as a matter of course, not caught up before an inspection.

If inspection prep currently feels like a sprint, that's the signal. The work is to build the systems that make preparedness the default.
